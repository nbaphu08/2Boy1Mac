## moon-apollo-11
You are standing in the Sea of Tranquility. In July 1969, two people walked here. When Eagle's upper half blasted back to orbit, its lower half stayed behind as a launch pad, and it is still here. A few steps away sits a panel of one hundred small mirrors. Scientists on Earth still bounce lasers off it today, making it the last working science experiment from the Apollo era. Look around you. Everything else here is silent. This one still answers.

## moon-surveyor-3
This is Surveyor 3, a robot sent to test the ground before astronauts arrived. It landed alone in the Ocean of Storms in 1967. Robot landers of that time had no rocket to come home, so it was built for a one-way trip. Two and a half years later, it had visitors. Apollo 12 set down just 155 meters away, and two astronauts walked over and took its camera home. It is the only time people have walked up to a robot they sent to another world.

## moon-apollo-15-lrv
This is the first car on the Moon. Apollo 15's rover carried Dave Scott and Jim Irwin 27.9 kilometers across the Hadley–Apennine region, far beyond where they could walk. Only the lander's small top half flew home, with no room for a car. So before leaving, Scott parked it about 150 meters away, its TV camera aimed so people on Earth could watch the liftoff live. It has been parked here ever since.

## moon-apollo-17
This is the valley of Taurus–Littrow. In December 1972, Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt explored it. Like every Apollo landing, only the crew cabin flew home. Everything else stayed: the base of their lander, their rover, their experiments, and the last human footprints on the Moon. Cameras in orbit can still see the rover tracks today. No one has walked on the Moon since.

## moon-grail
There is no machine standing here, only two small craters. They were made by twin spacecraft called Ebb and Flow. For most of 2012 they flew one behind the other around the Moon, measuring the tiny changes in distance between them to map the Moon's gravity. When their mission ended in December 2012, NASA steered them into this mountain near the north pole, on purpose. The site is named for Sally Ride, America's first woman in space.

## mars-viking-1
Welcome to Chryse Planitia, on Mars. This is Viking 1, the first American spacecraft to land on Mars. Minutes after touching down on the twentieth of July, 1976, it photographed its own footpad resting on Martian soil. It kept working for more than six years, reporting the weather and testing the soil for signs of life. No spacecraft has ever come back from Mars. Every machine you will find here was built for a one-way trip.

## mars-pathfinder
Pathfinder reached Mars on the fourth of July, 1997, wrapped in airbags. It bounced across the ground of Ares Vallis before rolling to a stop. Then its petals opened, and out drove Sojourner, NASA's first Mars rover. The mission was planned to last a week. It lasted 83 days. The little rover is still here, close to its lander.

## mars-spirit
This is Spirit, a six-wheeled robot geologist about the size of a golf cart. It landed in Gusev Crater in January 2004 and explored for six years. In 2007 it uncovered bright soil that was about ninety percent silica, a sign that water, perhaps volcanic steam, once soaked this ground. In 2009 it became trapped in soft sand at a place called Troy. It could no longer drive, so it kept doing science where it stood.

## mars-opportunity
This is Opportunity, Spirit's identical twin, on the other side of Mars. It was built to last 90 Martian days. It kept going for more than fourteen years, and drove 45.16 kilometers, longer than a marathon. Along the way it found tiny iron-rich spheres nicknamed blueberries, and mineral veins laid down by water long ago. It is still out there, at the edge of Endeavour Crater.

## mars-phoenix
This is Phoenix. It landed farther north than any spacecraft before it, and dug into the frozen ground of the Martian Arctic. It confirmed water ice just below the surface, and watched snow fall from the clouds. Phoenix was never designed to survive the polar winter. When the winter came, it fell silent. Images from orbit later showed its solar panels damaged, probably by the weight of frozen carbon dioxide.

## mars-insight
InSight did not drive anywhere. It sat still on the plains of Elysium, and listened. Its seismometer felt 1,319 marsquakes, letting scientists map the inside of the planet. Year by year, dust settled on its solar panels, until there was no longer enough power to keep listening. It is still here, exactly where it landed.

## mars-ingenuity
This small helicopter made history. Ingenuity was the first aircraft to make a powered, controlled flight on another planet. It was built to try up to five flights. It flew seventy-two. Tucked beneath its solar panel, it carried a small piece of fabric from the Wright brothers' 1903 Flyer, the first airplane on Earth. Today it rests in Jezero Crater, at a place its team called Valinor Hills.
